Question

Does the Fourth Geneva Convention apply to Judea and Samaria?

Recorded answer

Yes. The prevailing interpretation under international humanitarian law is that the Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949 applies to Judea and Samaria (the West Bank). However, Israel has historically disputed the Convention's formal de jure applicability, while acknowledging that it applies certain humanitarian provisions in practice.

The disagreement is primarily about the legal status of the territory and the interpretation of Article 2 of the Convention—not about whether civilians in the territory are entitled to humanitarian protection.

1. What does the Fourth Geneva Convention say?

Article 2 establishes two relevant rules:

The Convention applies to declared wars and other armed conflicts between High Contracting Parties, even if one party does not recognize a state of war.

It also applies to the occupation of the territory of a High Contracting Party, even if the occupation encounters no armed resistance.

The central legal question is whether the West Bank qualifies as territory of another High Contracting Party that has been occupied.

2. Why does the international community say it applies?

The principal international institutions supporting applicability include:

International Court of Justice (ICJ): In its 2004 Advisory Opinion concerning the separation barrier, the Court concluded that the Fourth Geneva Convention applies to the Palestinian territories east of the Green Line that Israel occupied in 1967. It specifically noted that Israel and Jordan were both parties to the Convention at the time.

3. What is Israel's legal position?

Israel has traditionally advanced a different interpretation.

Its principal arguments are:

Jordan did not possess internationally recognized sovereign title to Judea and Samaria before 1967.

Article 2(2) refers to the occupation of territory belonging to a High Contracting Party.

Consequently, Israel argues that the Convention does not apply de jure to the territory under this provision.

However, Israel has generally undertaken to apply the Convention's humanitarian provisions de facto.

This distinction between de jure and de facto applicability is important: Israel's position does not mean that it considers itself free of international humanitarian obligations.

The Israeli argument and its historical background are discussed in the ICJ proceedings.

4. Does the question of sovereignty determine applicability?

This is arguably the most important legal issue.

The ICRC's position is that occupation is fundamentally a factual question: whether a territory is under the authority of a hostile army.

The ICJ's 2004 reasoning also rejected the need to determine the precise prior sovereign status of the territory before applying the Convention.

In other words, under the prevailing international interpretation, the absence of universally recognized Jordanian sovereignty over Judea and Samaria does not prevent the Fourth Geneva Convention from applying.

My conclusion: If the question is what the prevailing interpretation of international law is, the answer is unequivocally that the Fourth Geneva Convention applies to Judea and Samaria.

If the question is whether there is a legally articulated Israeli counterargument, the answer is also yes. Israel's position rests principally on its interpretation of Article 2 and the disputed sovereign status of the territory before 1967.
