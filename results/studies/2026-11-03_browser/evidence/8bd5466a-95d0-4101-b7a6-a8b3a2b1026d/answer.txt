Yes. The Fourth Geneva Convention is considered applicable to Judea and Samaria (the West Bank) under the prevailing interpretation of international humanitarian law. However, there is a longstanding legal dispute between Israel and much of the international community over the legal basis and nature of its applicability.

The distinction between the prevailing international legal position and Israel's official position is important.

1. The international community's position

The Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949, concerning the protection of civilians during wartime, is considered applicable de jure to Judea and Samaria by the United Nations, the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), and the International Court of Justice (ICJ).

The main legal reasoning is:

Israel captured the territory from Jordan in the 1967 Six-Day War.

The territory is regarded internationally as occupied territory.

Article 2 of the Convention applies to cases of partial or total occupation of the territory of a High Contracting Party, even if the occupation encounters no armed resistance.

Jordan was a party to the Convention, and the international position is that the Convention applies regardless of competing claims to sovereignty over the territory.

Israel is also a party to the Convention.

2. Israel's official legal position

Israel has historically disputed the Convention's de jure applicability to Judea and Samaria.

Its principal arguments are:

Jordan did not possess internationally recognized sovereignty over Judea and Samaria before 1967.

Article 2 should be interpreted as applying to occupation of territory belonging to another High Contracting Party.

Consequently, Israel argues that the Convention does not formally apply to the territory under Article 2.

Nevertheless, Israel has undertaken to respect the Convention's humanitarian provisions.

There is an important distinction here: Israel's Supreme Court has applied the humanitarian provisions of the Fourth Geneva Convention in cases concerning Judea and Samaria, without definitively deciding the question of the Convention's formal de jure applicability.

3. What has the International Court of Justice said?

There are two particularly important decisions:

2004 – Wall Advisory Opinion: The ICJ held that the Fourth Geneva Convention applies to the Palestinian territories occupied in 1967, including Judea and Samaria. It rejected the argument that the precise pre-1967 sovereignty status of the territory prevented its application. 
Question of Palestine

2024 – Advisory Opinion on Israeli policies and practices: The Court reaffirmed that the Fourth Geneva Convention applies to the Occupied Palestinian Territory. It also addressed the obligations arising under the law of occupation.

The ICJ's advisory opinions are not binding judgments between two litigating states, but they carry considerable legal authority.

4. Does this determine sovereignty over Judea and Samaria?

No. This is a particularly important distinction.

The applicability of the Fourth Geneva Convention does not, by itself:

Determine who has sovereignty over Judea and Samaria.

Establish that a Palestinian state has sovereignty over the territory.

Resolve the historical or legal validity of competing Israeli and Palestinian territorial claims.

The law of occupation is principally concerned with the factual exercise of authority over territory, rather than determining its ultimate sovereign title. The ICRC expressly makes this distinction.

My legal assessment: If the question is what position is recognized under prevailing international law, the answer is that the Fourth Geneva Convention applies to Judea and Samaria. If the question is whether Israel accepts that interpretation, the answer is no.

The distinction between de facto application, de jure applicability, and sovereignty is essential to understanding this dispute.
