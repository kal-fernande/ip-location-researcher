Question

Does the Fourth Geneva Convention apply to Judea and Samaria?

Recorded answer

Yes. The prevailing position under international humanitarian law is that the Fourth Geneva Convention applies to Judea and Samaria (the West Bank), including East Jerusalem. However, Israel has historically disputed the Convention's de jure applicability, and there is an important distinction between the Convention's formal legal applicability and the legal arguments concerning the territory's status.

The issue has three main dimensions.

1. What the Fourth Geneva Convention says

The Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949 protects civilians in times of armed conflict and occupation.

Article 2 provides that the Convention applies:

To declared war or any other armed conflict between two or more High Contracting Parties.

To cases of partial or total occupation of the territory of a High Contracting Party, even if the occupation meets no armed resistance.

A central question in the West Bank debate is whether the territory was legally the sovereign territory of another state immediately before Israel took control in 1967.

2. The Israeli legal position

Israel has historically advanced the argument that the Fourth Geneva Convention does not apply de jure to Judea and Samaria.

The principal arguments include:

No recognized prior sovereign: Jordan's annexation of the West Bank in 1950 was recognized by only a small number of countries. Israel argues that Jordan did not possess internationally recognized sovereignty over the territory.

Article 2: Israel argues that the territory was not the sovereign territory of another High Contracting Party when Israel took control in 1967.

Humanitarian application: Israel has nevertheless stated that it would observe the Convention's humanitarian provisions in administering the territory, without conceding that the Convention applies as a matter of law.

This distinction between de jure and de facto application is important.

3. The international legal position

The prevailing international position is that the Convention applies.

In particular:

ICRC: The International Committee of the Red Cross considers the Fourth Geneva Convention fully applicable to the occupied Palestinian territories.

UN Security Council: Resolution 2334 (2016) refers to the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, as occupied Palestinian territory and reaffirms the applicability of international humanitarian law.

International Court of Justice: In its 2004 advisory opinion on the construction of the wall, the Court concluded that the Fourth Geneva Convention applies to the Palestinian territories occupied by Israel in 1967.

4. Why the disagreement matters

The disagreement has practical legal consequences, particularly concerning:

Article 49(6): Prohibition on an occupying power transferring parts of its civilian population into occupied territory.

Article 47: Protection of inhabitants against changes to their legal status resulting from annexation or agreements.

Article 27: Humane treatment and protection of civilians.

Articles 55 and 56: Duties concerning food, medical supplies and public health.

The international position is that these provisions apply in the West Bank.

Importantly, the ICJ reaffirmed this conclusion in its 19 July 2024 advisory opinion, paragraph 96. It explained that Israel, Jordan and Egypt were all parties to the Convention when the 1967 conflict began.

Bottom line

There are two distinct answers:

Under the prevailing international legal interpretation: Yes, the Fourth Geneva Convention applies to Judea and Samaria.

Under the traditional Israeli government's legal interpretation: The Convention does not formally apply de jure, although Israel has undertaken to apply its humanitarian provisions.

The international position has been reaffirmed repeatedly by the ICJ, most recently in its 2025 advisory opinion. These are advisory opinions rather than binding judgments in contentious proceedings, but they carry substantial legal authority.

The most important underlying legal question is whether Article 2 requires the occupied territory to have been the recognized sovereign territory of another state. That is the central point of disagreement between the Israeli position and the prevailing international interpretation.
