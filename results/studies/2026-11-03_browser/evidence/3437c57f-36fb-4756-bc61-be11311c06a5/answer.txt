Whether the Fourth Geneva Convention applies to Judea and Samaria (commonly referred to as the West Bank) is a subject of long-standing legal debate involving different interpretations of international law, state practice, and judicial rulings.

The two primary perspectives are divided between the consensus of the international community and the position held by the State of Israel.

1. The International Consensus and Majority Legal View

The overwhelming majority of the international community—including the United Nations Security Council, the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), and the International Court of Justice (ICJ)—maintains that the Fourth Geneva Convention does apply de jure (by law) to Judea and Samaria.

The Basis for Application: Article 2 of the Fourth Geneva Convention states that the treaty applies to all cases of declared war or armed conflict between two or more High Contracting Parties, and to "all cases of partial or total occupation of the territory of a High Contracting Party".

The "Missing Reversioner" Argument: Proponents of this view argue that Jordan’s control of the West Bank prior to 1967 (and Ottoman/British rule before that) was superseded by the occupation, and that the local Palestinian population constitutes "protected persons" under the Convention. Because Jordan was a High Contracting Party to the Geneva Conventions, the territory falls squarely under the definition of occupied territory of a High Contracting Party.

Key Implications: Under this framework, provisions such as Article 49—which prohibits an occupying power from deporting or transferring parts of its own civilian population into occupied territory—are held to apply, leading international bodies to view Israeli settlements in the area as a violation of international law. International judicial bodies, such as the ICJ in its 2004 and 2024 advisory opinions, have consistently affirmed this stance.

2. The Israeli Legal Position

The Government of Israel has traditionally maintained that the Fourth Geneva Convention does not apply de jure to Judea and Samaria, though it has adopted a policy of applying its humanitarian provisions de facto.

The Basis for Non-Application: Israeli legal arguments rest primarily on the wording of Article 2 of the Convention, which refers to the occupation of territory belonging to another "High Contracting Party". Israel argued that prior to 1967, Jordan’s sovereignty over Judea and Samaria was not widely recognized internationally (only recognized by a couple of nations, as Jordan's annexation in 1950 was rejected by most of the world). Therefore, because the territory was not legally part of a recognized sovereign state (High Contracting Party) prior to being administered by Jordan, the strict technical prerequisites for the Fourth Geneva Convention's application to occupied sovereign territory are not met.

The Humanitarian Undertaking: While contesting the de jure applicability of the Convention, successive Israeli governments have stated that the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) will voluntarily observe the humanitarian provisions of the Fourth Geneva Convention in their administration of the territories.

Domestic Judicial Stance: The Supreme Court of Israel has navigated this issue by ruling that while the Fourth Geneva Convention's technical/treaty-based framework may not be formally binding (de jure) due to the unique legal status of the territory, the humanitarian rules contained within it largely reflect customary international law, which the military administration is bound to respect.

Summary

International Community, ICJ, and ICRC: Yes, it applies de jure as occupied territory under the Fourth Geneva Convention.

Israel: No, it does not apply de jure because the territory was not previously recognized sovereign territory of a High Contracting Party, though its humanitarian provisions are observed de facto.
